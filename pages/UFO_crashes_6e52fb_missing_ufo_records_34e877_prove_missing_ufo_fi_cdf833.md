## Introduction

A missing government file is not automatically evidence of a UFO crash cover-up. However, investigators can often demonstrate that a specific document once existed even if the document itself has disappeared. In archival research, the strongest evidence frequently comes from the traces a file leaves behind: registry entries, file numbers, routing slips, references in letters, inventories, duplicate copies and records-management documentation. These surviving records allow researchers to distinguish between a document that probably never existed and one that almost certainly did.

Within investigations of alleged [UFO crashes]({{ 'ufo-crashes/' | relative_url }}) such as [Roswell]({{ 'roswell/' | relative_url }}) or Kecksburg, this distinction is important. A proven archival gap strengthens the case that the historical record is incomplete, but it does not establish what the missing document contained. The value of the evidence lies in showing that a known record has vanished and in narrowing the range of plausible explanations for its absence.

## How can a lost file be shown to have existed?

Archives are designed to track records throughout their life cycle. Even when the original file is gone, administrative systems often preserve evidence that it was created, circulated or transferred.

Investigators therefore look for independent documentary traces rather than relying on memories alone. The more unrelated sources that point to the same missing record, the stronger the conclusion that the document genuinely existed.

Typical forms of corroboration include:

* Incoming or outgoing correspondence referring to the document.
* Distribution lists showing who received copies.

* Duplicate or carbon copies retained in another office.

## Contemporaneous indexes and file registers

The strongest evidence usually comes from records created at the same time as the missing file itself.

Government agencies routinely maintained indexes, accession logs and filing systems so officials could retrieve documents efficiently. These administrative tools often survive even when individual files do not.

For example, a file register may record:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the assigned file number;
* the document's title or subject;
* the originating office;
* dates of creation and movement;
* later transfer or disposal information.

</div>

Because these registers were created for administrative purposes rather than public debate, historians generally regard them as more reliable than later recollections.

National archival guidance likewise requires detailed finding aids, folder lists and related documentation when permanent records are transferred. Those finding aids frequently become independent evidence that particular files once formed part of an official record series.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/records-mgmt/accessioning/finding-aid.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Finding Aid Requirements &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesFinding Aid Requirements &#124; National ArchivesSeptember 19, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 19, 2023</span></span></span>

In archival investigations concerning alleged UFO crashes, discovering a file number without the corresponding folder is often more significant than finding an isolated anecdote, because the numbering system demonstrates that an identifiable record occupied a defined place within an official filing structure.

## References preserved in surviving correspondence

A missing report often survives indirectly because other officials discussed it.

Letters, memoranda and routing slips may include wording such as:

* "As noted in report 17..."
* "Please see attached investigation."
* "Reference your memorandum of 8 July..."
* "Forwarding copy for review."

Even if the attachment has disappeared, the surrounding correspondence confirms that it once existed.

This kind of indirect documentation becomes especially persuasive when several offices independently refer to the same document. If headquarters, a field office and another agency each mention receiving the same report, the probability that the report was ever fictional becomes extremely small.

Investigators therefore reconstruct document histories by linking these scattered references into a chronological chain. Each surviving reference reduces uncertainty about whether a document was actually created.

## Copies, quotations and parallel filing systems

Government correspondence was rarely unique.

Many twentieth-century offices produced:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* carbon copies;
* office copies;
* distribution copies;
* microfilm reproductions;
* later certified copies.

</div>

A missing original may therefore survive elsewhere.

Investigators also compare parallel filing systems. One office may have destroyed its copy while another retained the identical correspondence. Military headquarters, intelligence agencies, local commands and civilian departments frequently maintained overlapping collections.

Sometimes only quotations survive. A later memorandum may reproduce several paragraphs from an earlier report now believed lost. Although incomplete, such quotations demonstrate both the report's existence and at least part of its contents.

This explains why archival researchers rarely stop searching after one unsuccessful records request. They follow every documented distribution path to determine whether another recipient preserved a duplicate.

## Records-management documentation can explain the disappearance

Once investigators establish that a file existed, the next question becomes why it is missing.

Records-management evidence is often more informative than speculation.

Researchers examine:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* approved disposal schedules;
* transfer forms;
* accession records;
* destruction certificates;
* retention authorities;
* archival correspondence concerning missing boxes.

</div>

If documentation shows that the file series was legally destroyed under an authorised retention schedule, the disappearance may reflect ordinary administration rather than concealment.

Conversely, if a register records a file that should have been transferred permanently but no transfer record or destruction authority exists, the gap deserves closer scrutiny.

The absence of required records-management paperwork is therefore evidence about recordkeeping, not necessarily about the underlying UFO claim.

## Why witness testimony alone is usually insufficient

Former officials sometimes recall preparing reports that cannot now be found.

Such testimony can be valuable, particularly when the witness had direct responsibility for creating or filing records. Nevertheless, historians generally seek documentary corroboration before concluding that a specific government file once existed.

Records officers, archivists and clerical staff can be especially important [witnesses]({{ 'witnesses/' | relative_url }}) because they explain:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* how documents were numbered;
* whether duplicate copies were routinely created;
* how transfers occurred;
* whether files were known to have been misplaced or destroyed.

</div>

When their descriptions match surviving registers or correspondence, confidence in the reconstructed record history increases substantially.

## What proving existence does—and does not—show

Demonstrating that a missing UFO-related file once existed is a meaningful archival finding, but its significance has clear limits.

It can show:

* an identifiable document was created;
* it occupied a recognised place within an official filing system;
* other officials referred to it;
* later recordkeeping became incomplete.

It cannot, by itself, prove:

* the file concerned an extraterrestrial craft;
* officials removed it to conceal extraordinary evidence;
* later [witness accounts]({{ 'witness-accounts/' | relative_url }}) accurately describe its contents.

For investigations into alleged UFO crashes, the strongest cases are therefore built from converging archival traces rather than from the absence of a single folder. A documented index entry, corroborating correspondence, surviving copies and consistent records-management evidence together can establish that a specific file once existed. What remains unresolved is the separate—and much harder—question of what that missing file actually contained.
