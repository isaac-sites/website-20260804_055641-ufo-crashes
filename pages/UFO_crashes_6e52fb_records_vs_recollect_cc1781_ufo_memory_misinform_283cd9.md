## Introduction

Later information can genuinely reshape how people remember an unusual event. In the context of alleged [UFO crashes]({{ 'ufo-crashes/' | relative_url }}), this means a witness who originally saw unfamiliar debris, lights or aircraft activity may later come to remember additional details after reading books, watching documentaries, discussing the event with others or being repeatedly interviewed. This does not necessarily imply dishonesty. Decades of cognitive psychology show that human memory is reconstructive rather than a literal recording, making sincere [witnesses]({{ 'witnesses/' | relative_url }}) vulnerable to incorporating post-event information into their recollections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/nature-index/topics/l4/misinformation-effects-on-eyewitness-memory" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Misinformation Effects on Eyewitness Memory &#124; Forensic Psychology &#124; Applied and Developmental Psychology &#124; Health sciences &#124; Topics...</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/UFO_crashes_6e52fb_records_vs_recollect_cc1781_ufo_memory_misinform_283cd9-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Memory Effects illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/UFO_crashes_6e52fb_records_vs_recollect_cc1781_ufo_memory_misinform_283cd9-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/UFO_crashes_6e52fb_records_vs_recollect_cc1781_ufo_memory_misinform_283cd9-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

This distinction is particularly important when comparing contemporary UFO reports with testimony collected many years later. As memories fade, external information can become intertwined with original perception, making it increasingly difficult to separate what was directly experienced from what was learned afterwards. For investigators, this means that confidence alone cannot establish historical accuracy.

## The misinformation effect in eyewitness recall

The best-established explanation for changing recollections is the **misinformation effect**. This describes the finding that information encountered after an event can alter later memory of that event. The effect was pioneered by psychologist Elizabeth Loftus and has since been replicated across many experimental settings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2522502/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Misinformation and memory: the creation of new memories</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Misinformation and memory: the creation of new memories - PubMed...</span></span></span>

Laboratory studies typically follow three stages:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. Participants observe an event.
2. They receive additional information, some of it inaccurate or suggestive.
3. They later recall what they experienced.

</div>

Those exposed to misleading information are consistently more likely to report details that never appeared in the original event. These false recollections are often expressed with considerable confidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/neuroscience/misinformation-effect" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Misinformation Effect</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Misinformation Effect - an overview &#124; ScienceDirect Topics...</span></span></span>

Applied to UFO crash claims, the mechanism suggests that a witness who originally remembered scattered wreckage might later recall exotic materials, military personnel or even non-human occupants if such details became familiar through repeated exposure after the event. Psychology cannot determine whether any particular UFO claim is true or false, but it explains why later testimony may differ substantially from contemporaneous accounts without requiring deliberate fabrication.

## How source-monitoring errors create borrowed memories

A closely related mechanism is **source monitoring**. People often remember information accurately while forgetting where it came from. Instead of recalling whether a detail was personally observed, read in a newspaper or heard during a conversation, the brain may retain only the detail itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/nature-index/topics/l4/misinformation-effects-on-eyewitness-memory" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Misinformation Effects on Eyewitness Memory &#124; Forensic Psychology &#124; Applied and Developmental Psychology &#124; Health sciences &#124; Topics...</span></span></span>

Over many years, this can blur several sources into one coherent narrative. A witness may unconsciously combine:

* original sensory experience;
* conversations with family or other witnesses;
* newspaper reports;

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* television documentaries;
* books or internet discussions;
* repeated interview questions.

</div>

Because these sources are integrated gradually, the resulting memory often feels internally consistent. The person may sincerely believe every element originated from direct observation when some details actually entered memory much later.

This process helps explain why accounts collected decades after an alleged UFO crash sometimes contain vivid descriptions absent from earlier statements. Rather than indicating deception, the memory may have become a reconstruction assembled from both perception and later information.

## Why repeated retelling strengthens altered memories

Memory does not simply fade with age. Every act of remembering also reconstructs the memory, creating opportunities for modification. Each interview, documentary or discussion effectively becomes another occasion to integrate new information into the remembered event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/nature-index/topics/l4/misinformation-effects-on-eyewitness-memory" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Misinformation Effects on Eyewitness Memory &#124; Forensic Psychology &#124; Applied and Developmental Psychology &#124; Health sciences &#124; Topics...</span></span></span>

Research suggests repetition itself is especially influential. Repeated exposure to the same claim can increase both acceptance of misinformation and confidence in it, even when the claim originates from a single source rather than many independent ones. Familiarity can therefore be mistaken for evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S000169181100223X" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Repetition, not number of sources, increases both susceptibility to misinformation and confidence in the accuracy of eyewitn...</span></span></span>

Within long-running UFO cases, this has practical consequences. Highly publicised narratives often circulate for decades through books, conferences, television programmes and online communities. Witnesses revisiting their memories repeatedly may unknowingly incorporate recurring themes until they become inseparable from the original experience.

## Why confidence does not guarantee accuracy

One of the most counter-intuitive findings in eyewitness research is that confidence and accuracy are not the same thing. People can express great certainty about memories that are demonstrably mistaken. Conversely, accurate memories may be reported hesitantly or incompletely.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/reports-suggested-memories-do-people-truly-believe-them-adult" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ojp.gov">[Office of Justice Programs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ojp.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">From Adult Eyewitness Testimony: Current Trends and Developments, P 3-26, 1994, David Frank Ross, et al, eds. -- See NCJ-159543) &#124; Offic...</span></span></span>

This matters because dramatic UFO testimony is often evaluated partly by the apparent sincerity of the witness. Psychological research indicates that sincerity should not be confused with historical reliability.

Investigators therefore place particular value on:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* statements recorded immediately after the event;
* original notebooks and interviews;
* dated photographs and documents;
* confidence recorded before repeated questioning;
* consistency across independent contemporary sources.

</div>

## What this means for historical UFO crash investigations

The reconstructive nature of memory does not prove that any specific UFO crash account is inaccurate. Some late witnesses may genuinely recall details omitted from early reports, while official records can themselves be incomplete or misleading.

Instead, cognitive psychology provides a framework for weighing evidence. Contemporary records establish what was documented before later narratives became available, while delayed testimony must be interpreted with awareness that post-event information may have reshaped sincere recollections.

For that reason, historians and investigators generally give greater evidential weight to accounts recorded close to the alleged event and treat later interviews as valuable but potentially contaminated by decades of discussion, media coverage and cultural storytelling. Understanding how post-event information rewrites memory helps explain why UFO [crash narratives]({{ 'crash-narrative/' | relative_url }}) can grow more detailed over time without requiring witnesses to be intentionally deceptive.
