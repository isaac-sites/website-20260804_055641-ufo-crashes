## Introduction

The underwater search that followed the 1967 Shag Harbour incident is one of the best-documented parts of the case because it involved an official Canadian military response rather than later speculation. After [witnesses]({{ 'witnesses/' | relative_url }}) reported lights descending into the sea and rescue crews failed to locate a crashed aircraft, the Canadian Navy deployed military divers to examine the seabed. Their task was straightforward: determine whether an aircraft, vessel or any other substantial object had come to rest beneath the reported impact area. After several days of systematic searching, the divers recovered no identifiable wreckage, no aircraft components, no bodies and no evidence confirming that a solid object had crashed into the seabed. That negative result remains one of the central facts of the [Shag Harbour mystery]({{ 'shag-harbour/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives/collection/research-help/science-technology/ufos.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s UFOs: The search for the unknown</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canada&#x27;s UFOs: The search for the unknown - Canada.caMarch 2, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 2, 2026</span></span></span>

## How the underwater search was organised

Once search-and-rescue authorities established that no civilian or military aircraft had been reported missing, the operation shifted from a surface rescue to an underwater recovery effort. The Royal Canadian Navy assigned a diving team from HMCS *Granby*—then responsible for mobile diving operations in Atlantic Canada—to investigate the area identified by witnesses and the earlier surface search. Contemporary and later military histories agree that the divers arrived within days of the incident and worked from support vessels while local boats assisted on the surface.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://navydiver.ca/Documents/History/RCN%20DIVING%20BRANCH%20HISTORY%20-%20Part%2014.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: navydiver.ca">[Canadian Naval Divers Association]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">navydiver.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Canadian Naval Divers Association Microsoft Word</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canadian Naval Divers AssociationMicrosoft Word - RCN DIVING BRANCH HISTORY - Part 14.docxDecember 16, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 16, 2016</span></span></span>

Unlike many later retellings, the documented purpose of the dive operation was not to recover an alien craft. The mission reflected standard emergency practice for a suspected [aircraft accident]({{ 'aircraft-accidents/' | relative_url }}) at sea. If an aircraft had entered the water, investigators expected to find at least some combination of:

* structural wreckage;
* fuel or oil [contamination]({{ 'contamination/' | relative_url }});

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* impact scars or disturbed seabed;
* personal effects or human remains; or
* identifiable mechanical components.

</div>

Finding any of these would have allowed investigators to identify the object or at least confirm that something substantial had reached the seabed.

Accounts compiled from official records and interviews indicate that the divers systematically examined the reported area using the diving equipment available in 1967, supported by drag searches and sonar-assisted localisation where practical. Although underwater search technology of the period was considerably less capable than modern side-scan sonar and remotely operated vehicles, it was adequate for locating the sort of debris field expected from a conventional aircraft accident in relatively shallow coastal water.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://theblackarchive.net/en/case/013" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackarchive.net">[검은 기록 보관소]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackarchive.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">검은 기록 보관소The Shag Harbour UFO Incident — CASE FILE 013 &#124; The Black Archive</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">검은 기록 보관소The Shag Harbour UFO Incident — CASE FILE 013 &#124; The Black Archive</span></span></span>

## The probable search area and its limits

The dive operation concentrated on the location indicated by the surface rescue effort rather than an unrestricted search of the wider Atlantic. Witnesses, RCMP officers and local fishermen had all converged on approximately the same offshore area where lights were reported to have descended and where yellowish foam had been observed floating on the surface. That location became the primary underwater search zone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://theblackarchive.net/en/case/013" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackarchive.net">[검은 기록 보관소]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackarchive.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">검은 기록 보관소The Shag Harbour UFO Incident — CASE FILE 013 &#124; The Black Archive</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">검은 기록 보관소The Shag Harbour UFO Incident — CASE FILE 013 &#124; The Black Archive</span></span></span>

Several practical limitations should also be recognised when interpreting the results.

First, witness estimates of distance over open water at night are inherently imprecise. Even when multiple observers broadly agree on direction, estimating an exact splash point is difficult because darkness, lack of fixed reference points and changing viewing angles introduce uncertainty.

Second, the seabed itself is not perfectly uniform. Sand, gravel, rock and uneven terrain can complicate underwater searches, particularly using the equipment available in the late 1960s.

Third, search planners had no confirmed crash coordinates because there had been no radar-confirmed aircraft accident. The divers were searching a probable area derived from eyewitness observations rather than a precisely known impact position.

These limitations mean the operation cannot prove that absolutely nothing entered the water. They do, however, establish that investigators found nothing within the area judged most likely to contain a wreck if one had existed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://theblackarchive.net/en/case/013" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackarchive.net">[검은 기록 보관소]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackarchive.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">검은 기록 보관소The Shag Harbour UFO Incident — CASE FILE 013 &#124; The Black Archive</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">검은 기록 보관소The Shag Harbour UFO Incident — CASE FILE 013 &#124; The Black Archive</span></span></span>

## What the divers actually found

The most important point is also the simplest: the military divers did not recover any object that identified the source of the reported lights.

Across official summaries and subsequent historical reviews, the reported findings are remarkably consistent:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* no aircraft wreckage;
* no ship debris;
* no metallic fragments attributable to a crash;
* no fuel or oil slick associated with a submerged vehicle;
* no human remains; and
* no identifiable object resting on the seabed within the searched area.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://theblackarchive.net/en/case/013" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackarchive.net">[theblackarchive.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackarchive.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">검은 기록 보관소The Shag Harbour UFO Incident — CASE FILE 013 &#124; The Black Archive</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">검은 기록 보관소The Shag Harbour UFO Incident — CASE FILE 013 &#124; The Black Archive</span></span></span>

</div>

This absence of physical evidence distinguishes the dive search from many aviation recovery operations. Aircraft accidents at sea often leave extensive debris, floating material or underwater wreckage even when the fuselage itself is difficult to locate. In the Shag Harbour case, none of those expected indicators were documented during the military search.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://theblackarchive.net/en/case/013" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackarchive.net">[검은 기록 보관소]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackarchive.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">검은 기록 보관소The Shag Harbour UFO Incident — CASE FILE 013 &#124; The Black Archive</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">검은 기록 보관소The Shag Harbour UFO Incident — CASE FILE 013 &#124; The Black Archive</span></span></span>

## What nil results do and do not mean

The unsuccessful dive operation has generated competing interpretations for decades, but the evidence supports only a limited number of conclusions.

The search **does support** the following:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Canadian authorities considered the reports credible enough to justify deploying military divers.
* A genuine underwater recovery operation took place.
* No identifiable wreckage was recovered during that operation.
* No official investigation concluded that an alien spacecraft had been located or recovered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives/collection/research-help/science-technology/ufos.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s UFOs: The search for the unknown</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canada&#x27;s UFOs: The search for the unknown - Canada.caMarch 2, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 2, 2026</span></span></span>

The search **does not support** several claims that often appear in later UFO literature.

There is no official evidence that divers recovered extraterrestrial technology, encountered an intact craft on the seabed or participated in a secret recovery operation whose findings were later concealed. Those narratives stem primarily from much later witness recollections, secondary investigations and speculative accounts rather than from the contemporaneous military records describing the dive search itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives/collection/research-help/science-technology/ufos.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[canada.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s UFOs: The search for the unknown</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canada&#x27;s UFOs: The search for the unknown - Canada.caMarch 2, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 2, 2026</span></span></span>

Likewise, the failure to find debris does not by itself prove that no unusual event occurred. A negative search result simply means investigators were unable to locate physical evidence capable of identifying the reported object. The lights witnessed that night therefore remain [unexplained]({{ 'unexplained/' | relative_url }}), but the underwater search provides no direct physical support for claims of an extraterrestrial crash.

## Why the dive search remains important

Although the divers found nothing identifiable, the operation remains a key part of the Shag Harbour case because it transformed a local sighting into a documented government investigation. Unlike many UFO reports that end with witness testimony alone, Shag Harbour produced an official sequence of rescue operations, military involvement and recorded search activity.

For historians, the significance lies less in what was recovered than in what was not. The Canadian military searched the most probable underwater location expecting to solve what initially appeared to be an ordinary aviation emergency. Instead, the operation concluded without finding any wreckage capable of explaining the reported descent into the sea. That absence of evidence has remained one of the defining—and most carefully documented—features of the Shag Harbour incident.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives/collection/research-help/science-technology/ufos.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[canada.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s UFOs: The search for the unknown</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canada&#x27;s UFOs: The search for the unknown - Canada.caMarch 2, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 2, 2026</span></span></span>
