## Introduction

Glenn Dennis became one of the most influential [witnesses]({{ 'witnesses/' | relative_url }}) in the [Roswell]({{ 'roswell/' | relative_url }}) story because he claimed that a military nurse privately described seeing the autopsy of recovered non-human bodies. That account helped connect otherwise separate rumours into a single narrative involving a crash, recovered bodies and a military cover-up. Yet the nurse's story remains unverified despite decades of investigation. The central problems are not simply that the alleged witness never testified publicly, but that the nurse's identity repeatedly changed, no contemporary military records confirm her existence under the names Dennis supplied, and no independent documentation places such events at the Roswell Army Air Field hospital in July 1947. These weaknesses have led not only sceptics but also several prominent Roswell researchers who otherwise accepted parts of the Roswell case to conclude that Dennis's nurse story cannot presently be verified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/FOID/Reading%20Room/UFOsandUAPs/RoswellReportCaseClosed.pdf?ver=2017-05-22-113519-430" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[DLA Energy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DLA Energy The Cover</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A solarized image taken from a U.S. AirSeptember 26, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 26, 2012</span></span></span>

## What Glenn Dennis claimed happened

Dennis worked for Ballard Funeral Home in Roswell in 1947 and did have genuine connections with personnel at the local Army Air Field hospital. His account, first publicised more than forty years after the alleged events, claimed that military personnel telephoned the funeral home asking about unusually small hermetically sealed coffins and methods for preserving badly damaged bodies. He also said that, after delivering a serviceman to the base hospital, he encountered unusual military activity and later met privately with an Army nurse who described having briefly witnessed doctors examining several small, badly damaged bodies unlike normal human remains. According to Dennis, the nurse had even sketched what she saw before being abruptly transferred away.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gutenberg.org/files/63659/63659-h/63659-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

The nurse's alleged testimony became the critical link in Dennis's narrative. Dennis himself did not claim to have seen [alien bodies]({{ 'alien-bodies/' | relative_url }}). Instead, virtually all of the extraordinary details depended upon what this unnamed—or later differently named—nurse supposedly told him. That makes her account the essential supporting witness rather than a minor detail.

## The false name and failed verification

The most persistent problem is the identity of the nurse.

When researchers first interviewed Dennis in the late 1980s, he identified her as "Naomi Self" or "Naomi Maria Selff." Investigators, including researchers sympathetic to the Roswell crash hypothesis, searched Army personnel files, hospital rosters and other military records for someone matching that identity. They found no evidence that any Army nurse by that name served at Roswell Army Air Field, nor even that such a military nurse existed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/FOID/Reading%20Room/UFOsandUAPs/RoswellReportCaseClosed.pdf?ver=2017-05-22-113519-430" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[DLA Energy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DLA Energy The Cover</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A solarized image taken from a U.S. AirSeptember 26, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 26, 2012</span></span></span>

Dennis later acknowledged that the name he had supplied was not genuine, explaining that he had altered it to protect the nurse's identity. He subsequently suggested other names, including "Naomi Sipes," but those identities also failed to produce verifiable military records or an independently confirmed witness. Each revision made historical verification more difficult because investigators could no longer test a fixed claim against documentary evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roswell_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Roswell incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Roswell incident</span></span></span>

The changing identity also complicated related parts of the story. Dennis variously claimed that the nurse had been transferred overseas, died in an [aircraft accident]({{ 'aircraft-accidents/' | relative_url }}), or disappeared from contact. Investigators searched military personnel records and reports relating to those claims but were unable to confirm them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/FOID/Reading%20Room/UFOsandUAPs/RoswellReportCaseClosed.pdf?ver=2017-05-22-113519-430" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[DLA Energy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DLA Energy The Cover</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A solarized image taken from a U.S. AirSeptember 26, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 26, 2012</span></span></span>

## Why one missing witness weakens the chain

The weakness of Dennis's story is not simply the absence of one witness. It is that nearly every extraordinary element depends on that witness alone.

Without the nurse, there is no first-hand account connecting the hospital to alleged alien bodies. Dennis's own observations consist mainly of unusual telephone enquiries, military presence and conversations. Those events, even if accepted, do not independently establish that extraterrestrial bodies were present.

From a historical perspective, this creates several evidential problems:

* **No direct testimony.** The nurse never appeared publicly, gave no recorded interview and left no authenticated written statement.
* **No contemporary documentation.** No surviving July 1947 hospital records, reports or correspondence describe the alleged autopsy events.
* **No stable identity.** The inability to establish who the nurse actually was prevents investigators from examining her service record or interviewing colleagues who definitely worked with her.
* **No independent corroboration.** Other alleged witnesses emerged years later, but none independently confirmed Dennis's specific meeting with the nurse or her claimed sketches and descriptions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/FOID/Reading%20Room/UFOsandUAPs/RoswellReportCaseClosed.pdf?ver=2017-05-22-113519-430" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[DLA Energy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DLA Energy The Cover</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A solarized image taken from a U.S. AirSeptember 26, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 26, 2012</span></span></span>

Because the entire hospital narrative depends upon an unverifiable intermediary, historians cannot treat it as independently established evidence.

## Military record searches found conflicting evidence

One reason the nurse story remains controversial is that it was subjected to unusually detailed documentary investigation.

During preparation of the U.S. Air Force's Roswell investigations, researchers examined morning reports and personnel records for the Roswell Army Air Field hospital. Those records showed that only a small number of Army nurses were assigned there during July 1947. The documented personnel movements did not match Dennis's account of a nurse being suddenly removed immediately after the alleged incident. Researchers also found no nurse corresponding to the names Dennis initially supplied.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/FOID/Reading%20Room/UFOsandUAPs/RoswellReportCaseClosed.pdf?ver=2017-05-22-113519-430" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[DLA Energy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DLA Energy The Cover</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A solarized image taken from a U.S. AirSeptember 26, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 26, 2012</span></span></span>

The investigation also examined Dennis's recollections about another nurse, "Captain Wilson" (nicknamed "Slatts"), whom he claimed helped arrange contact with the missing nurse. Personnel records again failed to match Dennis's description as given. ## Even supportive Roswell researchers became sceptical

The evolution of opinion among Roswell researchers is significant because criticism did not come solely from debunkers.

Kevin Randle, who spent years investigating Roswell from the perspective that something unusual may indeed have occurred, eventually concluded that Dennis's account was unreliable, largely because of the shifting identity of the nurse and other inconsistencies. Karl Pflock, another major Roswell investigator, likewise judged Dennis's story to be unsupported despite accepting that Dennis genuinely worked as a mortician in Roswell.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2022/07/understanding-roswell-addendum-1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[kevinrandle.blogspot.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">A Different Perspective: Understanding Roswell</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Addendum 1...</span></span></span>

This distinction matters. Researchers generally accept that Dennis was a real funeral home employee who interacted with military personnel. The dispute concerns whether the extraordinary claims added decades later accurately describe events from July 1947. Many investigators have separated those two questions rather than rejecting every aspect of Dennis's recollections outright.

## Why the story remains historically unverified

The nurse story occupies an unusual place within Roswell history because it illustrates how a compelling narrative can depend on a single missing link.

Historians normally evaluate witness testimony by asking whether identities can be established, statements compared with contemporary records and claims independently corroborated. Dennis's account performs poorly on all three measures. The key witness cannot be securely identified, the supplied names changed over time, documentary searches have failed to confirm the central claims, and no contemporary evidence places alien autopsies at the Roswell hospital.

That does not prove Dennis intentionally fabricated every detail or that every unusual event he remembered was false. Memories recorded more than forty years after an event can contain genuine recollections mixed with errors, assumptions or later influences. However, because the central nurse remains unverified and her alleged testimony cannot be independently examined, the story continues to be regarded as an unconfirmed—and increasingly disputed—component of the broader Roswell recovery narrative rather than established historical evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/FOID/Reading%20Room/UFOsandUAPs/RoswellReportCaseClosed.pdf?ver=2017-05-22-113519-430" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[whs.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DLA Energy The Cover</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A solarized image taken from a U.S. AirSeptember 26, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 26, 2012</span></span></span>
