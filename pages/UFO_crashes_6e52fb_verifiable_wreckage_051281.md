## Introduction

No alleged UFO-crash wreckage is publicly verifiable because no specimen currently combines the things needed to establish what it is and where it came from: documented recovery at a specific incident, an unbroken [chain of custody]({{ 'chain-of-custody/' | relative_url }}), unrestricted access for independent laboratories, and reproducible results that exclude terrestrial manufacture. Famous stories often contain testimony that material was removed, hidden or transferred into classified programmes, but the claimed craft and [recovery records]({{ 'recovery-record/' | relative_url }}) have not been produced for public examination.

A few privately held fragments have undergone sophisticated testing. So far, however, those tests have identified familiar elements, alloys or industrial processes rather than technology that could be shown to be extraterrestrial. This does not prove that every crash-retrieval allegation is false. It means that the evidence available to the public cannot distinguish hidden alien wreckage from misidentified debris, folklore, hearsay, hoaxes or secret but human technology. ## Claims of removed or hidden debris

The most common explanation for missing wreckage is that authorities recovered it before civilians or independent scientists could inspect it. In this version of events, military teams secure the site, remove every substantial component and classify the associated photographs, reports and laboratory work. The absence of public material is then treated not as a weakness in the crash claim, but as evidence that the concealment succeeded.

That reasoning is difficult to test because it makes the claim resistant to disconfirmation. No wreckage can be explained by a perfect clean-up; no records can be explained by classification or destruction; and [witnesses]({{ 'witnesses/' | relative_url }}) who cannot provide documents can be said to have been compartmentalised. Secrecy is certainly real around intelligence collection and experimental aerospace systems, but the existence of secrecy does not authenticate a particular story about a non-human craft.

[Roswell]({{ 'roswell/' | relative_url }}) illustrates the problem. The recovery itself is not disputed: personnel from Roswell Army Air Field collected debris in New Mexico in July 1947. Yet later accounts expanded from a modest debris field into stories involving large quantities of exotic metal, additional crash sites, bodies and aircraft loads of material. The US Air Force’s historical review noted this growth and contrasted it with early descriptions of sticks, paper, tape, rubber and foil-like material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/FOID/Reading%20Room/UFOsandUAPs/rosswe1.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[Defense Logistics Agency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Defense Logistics Agency Report of Air Force Research Regarding the &quot;Roswell</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Defense Logistics AgencyReport of Air Force Research Regarding the &quot;Roswell...September 26, 2012 — 27 Jul 1994 — Most versions now claim...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 26, 2012</span></span></span>

A 1995 Government Accountability Office search found only two contemporary federal records specifically connected with the Roswell event: a unit history and an FBI teletype. It also discovered that some administrative records for the relevant period had been destroyed without surviving information identifying when, why or by whom. That missing documentation reasonably creates suspicion, but it does not establish what the destroyed files contained. The records that remain do not authenticate the recovery of an extraterrestrial vehicle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-95-187.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Government Accountability Office NSIAD-95-187 Government Records</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeNSIAD-95-187 Government RecordsJuly 28, 1995 — 28 Jul 1995 — Our search for government records concerning...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 1995</span></span></span>

More recent allegations follow a similar pattern. In written testimony to a US House hearing in July 2023, former intelligence official David Grusch said that he had been informed of a long-running crash-retrieval and reverse-engineering programme to which he was denied access. His statement was significant as an allegation made under oath, but it did not place wreckage, authenticated recovery files or laboratory data into the public record. Public testimony that sources described a programme is evidence that the allegation exists; it is not physical verification of the programme’s supposed holdings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://oversight.house.gov/hearing/unidentified-anomalous-phenomena-implications-on-national-security-public-safety-and-government-transparency/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oversight.house.gov">[House Oversight Committee]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oversight.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">David Grusch</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">House Oversight CommitteeUnidentified Anomalous Phenomena: Implications on National Security, Public Safety, and Government Transparency...</span></span></span>

The US Department of Defense’s All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office, or AARO, later reported that its review had found no empirical evidence that a government, academic or private investigation had confirmed extraterrestrial technology. AARO said it examined classified and unclassified records, interviewed people claiming knowledge of retrieval activities and checked alleged programmes with officials responsible for sensitive-access oversight. Its findings are an official assessment rather than proof that concealment is impossible, but they provide no public confirmation that inspectable alien wreckage has been located.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/AARO_Historical_Record_Report_Vol_1_2024.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">AARO Historical Record Report Vol</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO Historical Record Report Vol</span></span></span>

One revealing example is KONA BLUE, a proposed Department of Homeland Security special-access programme frequently described in later rumours as a cover for recovered non-human technology. Released records show that supporters wanted a protected mechanism for studying supposed material, but the proposal was never formally established, received no funding and possessed no wreckage. The existence of paperwork discussing a possible retrieval programme was subsequently retold as evidence that an operational retrieval programme already held craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/UAP_Records_Research/AARO_DHS_Kona_Blue.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DHS Kona Blue</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DHS Kona Blue</span></span></span>

This distinction recurs throughout the subject:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* A **proposal to receive anomalous material** is not evidence that material was received.
* A **classified aerospace programme** is not necessarily a UFO programme.
* A **witness who heard about wreckage** is not a witness who inspected it.
* A **missing file** does not reveal its own contents.
* An official refusal to discuss a programme may protect ordinary national-security information without confirming an extraordinary interpretation.

</div>

The concealment hypothesis therefore remains possible in the abstract but publicly unverified in every specific case. Its central evidence is usually testimony about inaccessible evidence rather than the evidence itself.

## Why privately held fragments remain inconclusive

Small pieces of metal, slag, stone and layered material are sometimes presented as the surviving physical residue of UFO incidents. These objects appear more testable than memories because laboratories can measure their elemental composition, microscopic structure and isotopic ratios. Yet laboratory analysis answers only what a submitted specimen is made of. It cannot, by itself, prove that the specimen came from the alleged crash site.

This is the importance of provenance: the documented history of an object from discovery to examination. A meaningful sample would ideally be photographed where it was found, mapped in relation to other debris, collected with uncontaminated tools, sealed, labelled and transferred through recorded custodians. The National Institute of Standards and Technology defines chain of custody as documentation tracking evidence through collection, safeguarding, transfer and analysis, including who handled it, when and for what purpose. Forensic standards also emphasise preservation, controlled storage and retention for later reanalysis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://csrc.nist.gov/glossary/term/chain_of_custody" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: csrc.nist.gov">[nist.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">csrc.nist.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Computer Security Resource Centerchain of custody</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Computer Security Resource Centerchain of custody</span></span></span>

Most alleged UFO fragments do not meet that standard. They may have been kept for years in a witness’s home, passed between investigators, separated from the larger mass, or acquired from an owner who heard that someone else recovered them. Even an honest custodian may be unable to demonstrate that a tested fragment is the same material allegedly seen falling from an unusual object decades earlier.

The layered magnesium, zinc and bismuth specimen sometimes called “Art’s Parts” demonstrates the divide between an interesting object and verifiable wreckage. It has been publicly alleged to originate from a crashed extraterrestrial vehicle in or around 1947 and has attracted claims that its layers possess unusual wave-guiding or antigravity properties. AARO commissioned Oak Ridge National Laboratory to examine it using modern analytical methods.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/Information%20Papers/ORNL-Synopsis_Analysis_of_a_Metallic_Specimen.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Synopsis: Analysis of a Metallic Specimen</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Synopsis: Analysis of a Metallic Specimen</span></span></span>

The laboratory found a manufactured terrestrial alloy. Its constituent elements and isotopic composition were consistent with Earth-based sources, and the structure did not demonstrate the claimed extraordinary function. The analysis could characterise the submitted piece, but it could not restore the missing recovery history tying it to a spacecraft. A technologically unusual-looking specimen had become scientifically ordinary without resolving how the story became attached to it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/Information%20Papers/ORNL-Synopsis_Analysis_of_a_Metallic_Specimen.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Synopsis: Analysis of a Metallic Specimen</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Synopsis: Analysis of a Metallic Specimen</span></span></span>

The 1977 [Council Bluffs]({{ 'council-bluffs/' | relative_url }}), Iowa, incident presents a different difficulty. Witnesses reported a luminous aerial object, and responders found a hot mass of metal in a park. Investigators retained samples, making it one of the better-known cases involving actual material rather than a purely retrospective claim. Analyses identified iron-rich material resembling an earthly steel or cast-iron mixture, with slag and ash components. Later researchers argued that the circumstances of deposition remained puzzling, but the composition itself did not demonstrate exotic manufacture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/am/pii/S0376042121000907" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span>

Council Bluffs is worth distinguishing from invented “alien metal” stories because there really was material associated in time and place with an unusual report. Even so, the evidential leap remains large. Investigators would need to show not merely that a metallic mass was strange or difficult to explain, but that it came from a vehicle, that the reported aerial object deposited it, and that its properties could not result from industrial material, thermite, deliberate placement or another terrestrial process. An unresolved deposition mechanism does not transform familiar metal into extraterrestrial wreckage.

Chemical novelty can also be overstated. A sample need not contain a previously unknown element to be important; advanced engineering may use ordinary elements in unusual structures. But unusual layering, purity or isotope ratios are not self-interpreting. They must be compared with aerospace alloys, metallurgical by-products, experimental manufacturing techniques, [contamination]({{ 'contamination/' | relative_url }}) and measurement uncertainty. The peer-reviewed study of alleged anomalous materials by Garry Nolan, Jacques Vallée and colleagues argued that improved isotopic and microscopic techniques could usefully investigate such specimens, while stopping short of identifying any tested sample as extraterrestrial technology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/am/pii/S0376042121000907" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span>

Private ownership creates further obstacles. Owners may permit only limited testing, withhold part of the sample, restrict destructive analysis or provide material to laboratories under confidential arrangements. Results may be announced through interviews or documentaries without complete raw data, calibration information and control samples. Independent researchers then cannot reproduce the work or establish whether different laboratories tested material from the same object.

A fragment becomes publicly verifiable not when an analyst says it is unusual, but when qualified teams can inspect equivalent samples, review the complete methods and obtain compatible results.

## Why photographs and documents do not substitute for wreckage

Crash narratives often include photographs of debris, memoranda referring to discs, or claims that classified recovery records exist. Such records can establish that an incident was reported or investigated, but they usually cannot establish the physical identity of missing material.

A photograph may show shape, scale and surface appearance, yet cannot reliably determine isotopic composition, internal structure, manufacturing method or mechanical properties. It is also vulnerable to uncertain dates, locations and captions. Without the photographed object, analysts cannot test whether an apparently exotic surface is unusual metal, foil, insulation, scorched industrial waste or an ordinary component seen under poor conditions.

Documents pose a related authentication problem. A contemporary recovery log with names, serial numbers, map coordinates and transfer receipts would be highly valuable. By contrast, an undated memo of uncertain origin, a later transcription or a report that repeats another investigator’s account adds little physical verification. Multiple documents can appear mutually corroborating while ultimately tracing back to the same unverified witness or publication.

AARO described this problem as circular reporting: claims repeated among researchers, contractors and officials may acquire an appearance of independent confirmation even when they derive from a small number of original stories. Its historical review concluded that some allegations about hidden programmes arose from misidentified authentic programmes, proposed projects that never became operational, or second-hand accounts repeated without new evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://media.defense.gov/2024/Mar/08/2003409233/-1/-1/0/DOPSR-2024-0263-AARO-HISTORICAL-RECORD-REPORT-VOLUME-1-2024.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.defense.gov">[U.S. Department of War]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DOPSR 2024 0263 AARO HISTORICAL RECORD REPORT VOLUME</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DOPSR 2024 0263 AARO HISTORICAL RECORD REPORT VOLUME</span></span></span>

Classification makes assessment harder but does not reverse the burden of proof. A classified record could contain decisive evidence, mundane sensor capabilities, foreign intelligence, speculation or nothing directly relevant to a crash. Until its contents or associated material are made available to authorised independent examiners, its evidential value to the public remains indeterminate.

## What public verification would require

A credible wreckage case would have to survive two different tests. First, the material must be securely connected to the alleged incident. Second, its physical characteristics must support an origin that cannot reasonably be explained by known terrestrial or natural sources. Passing only one test is insufficient.

A strong recovery would require:

1. **A documented site.** Investigators would record precise coordinates, debris distribution, impact features, environmental conditions and the relationship between each object and the surrounding ground.

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

2. **Contemporaneous records.** Photographs, video, witness statements, collection notes and instrument readings would be created at the time, not reconstructed decades later.
3. **A protected chain of custody.** Every transfer, opening, subdivision and test would be logged. Seals and identifiers would make substitution or contamination detectable.
4. **Representative samples.** Researchers would examine multiple pieces from different parts of the debris field rather than a single fragment whose relationship to the larger object is unknown.
5. **Independent laboratory access.** Several competent institutions would receive blinded or matched portions, with enough material reserved for later replication.
6. **Transparent methods and controls.** Reports would include raw measurements, uncertainty ranges, calibration standards, instrument settings and comparisons with known industrial and aerospace materials.
7. **Evidence of manufacture and function.** An unusual composition alone would be less persuasive than integrated structures showing deliberate engineering, consistent performance and a function incompatible with accidental slag or ordinary debris.
8. **Serious exclusion of terrestrial sources.** Analysts would compare the wreckage against aircraft, balloons, rockets, satellites, weapons, industrial waste, meteorites and classified human technologies where authorised access allowed.
9. **Reproducible extraordinary findings.** Independent teams would need to confirm the same anomalous properties rather than merely agree that the object’s history was mysterious.
10. **Preservation for continuing scrutiny.** The bulk material, site records and analytical specimens would remain available to qualified researchers as techniques improved.

</div>

NASA’s UAP study emphasised a comparable principle for observations: reliable conclusions require calibrated instruments, standardised collection, multiple forms of corroboration and transparent analysis. Its central concern was not that anomalous reports should be ignored, but that poor and inconsistent data prevent firm conclusions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceIndependent Study Team ReportTo date, in the peer-reviewed scientific literature, there is no conclusive evidence suggesting...</span></span></span>

Purpose-built scientific projects have adopted the same logic. The Galileo Project’s proposed observatories combine wide- and narrow-field cameras, radar, spectral measurements, acoustic instruments and environmental sensors so that apparent anomalies can be checked across independent systems. The aim is to recognise artefacts and produce detections that others can verify. A crash investigation would need an equally integrated approach linking observations in the sky to material recovered on the ground.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18566" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

## The evidence gap is more specific than “no evidence”

It is too broad to say that UFO-crash stories have produced no physical evidence at all. Some incidents involve real debris, scorched ground, metallic samples or official recovery activity. The narrower and more accurate conclusion is that no publicly available specimen has been authenticated as wreckage from an extraterrestrial craft.

The failure usually occurs at one of three links:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The **incident is documented**, but the original debris is unavailable.
* The **sample is available**, but its connection to the incident is poorly documented.
* The **sample and history are partly documented**, but testing finds terrestrial material or no uniquely non-terrestrial property.

</div>

These gaps cannot be repaired by adding more testimony around the same missing object. Nor does an unexplained residue automatically inherit the most extraordinary interpretation of the sighting associated with it. “Unknown source”, “unresolved event” and “extraterrestrial technology” are different conclusions requiring progressively stronger evidence.

[Official reviews]({{ 'official-reviews/' | relative_url }}) also have limits. AARO’s inability to find verified off-world material does not logically demonstrate that none could exist in an undiscovered or inaccessible programme. Conversely, allegations that programmes are hidden do not establish their existence. The public position remains an evidential stalemate only at the level of abstract possibility. At the level of inspectable data, the balance is clearer: available samples have not provided reproducible proof of non-human manufacture.

The missing element is therefore not simply a spectacular piece of metal. It is a complete evidential package connecting place, object, custody, analysis and independent replication. Until an alleged recovery supplies that package, UFO wreckage may remain an object of testimony and suspicion, but not a publicly verified physical discovery.

    return url + joiner + params.join('&');
  }
  function domainLabel(marketId) {
    var cfg = markets[marketId] || markets[defaultMarket] || {};
    return cfg.domain_label || 'eBay';
  }
  function flagClass(marketId) {
    return 'fr-ebay-market-flag fr-ebay-market-flag--' + String(marketId || '').toLowerCase().replace(/_/g, '-').replace(/[^a-z0-9-]+/g, '');
  }
  function closeMenu(section) {
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var picker = section.querySelector('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
    if (menu) menu.hidden = true;
    if (trigger) trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', 'false');
    if (picker) picker.classList.remove('fr-ebay-market-picker--open');
  }
  function availableMarkets(section) {
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      return Array.prototype.slice.call(select.options)
        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
