## Introduction

An unresolved [unidentified]({{ 'unidentified/' | relative_url }}) anomalous phenomenon (UAP) report does not necessarily establish that a camera or sensor recorded a real physical object. In some cases, the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) concludes that the available evidence cannot even answer that basic question. Rather than identifying an aircraft, balloon or unknown craft, analysts may be left deciding whether the recorded signature represents a genuine object, a thermal reflection, an atmospheric temperature contrast, or a sensor artefact. This distinction is especially important in discussions of alleged [UFO crashes]({{ 'ufo-crashes/' | relative_url }}). Before investigators can ask what an object was or where it came from, they must first determine whether a physical object was present at all. Official AARO case summaries repeatedly show that insufficient sensor data can prevent that determination, leaving a report unresolved without implying an extraordinary explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/UAP-Cases/Official-UAP-Imagery/quot/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UAP Imagery</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO UAP Imagery...</span></span></span>

## Why some signatures cannot be attributed

Modern military sensors are designed for operational tasks such as surveillance, targeting and navigation rather than scientific investigation of unusual aerial events. They can produce images that appear compelling but lack the information needed to establish what the sensor actually detected.

Infrared systems, for example, measure differences in thermal radiation rather than producing ordinary photographs. A bright or dark patch in an infrared display does not automatically correspond to a discrete object. It may instead represent:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* a genuine object emitting heat;
* sunlight reflected from a surface;
* reflected thermal radiation;
* a natural temperature difference in clouds, air or terrain;
* image-processing artefacts within the sensor itself.

</div>

Because these possibilities can produce similar visual signatures, analysts often require additional measurements before concluding that a physical object existed. AARO has published several unresolved cases where it explicitly states that the observed heat signature has characteristics consistent with a physical object but cannot determine whether the signature originated from an actual object or from another source.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/UAP-Cases/Official-UAP-Imagery/quot/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UAP Imagery</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO UAP Imagery...</span></span></span>

This is a more limited conclusion than saying "the object is unidentified". Instead, the uncertainty begins one step earlier: investigators cannot confidently establish that the recorded signal represents an object in physical space.

## Reflections, temperature differences and display errors

AARO's published unresolved imagery provides a useful illustration of how analysts distinguish between uncertain detections and confirmed objects.

In multiple infrared cases from Africa (2022 and 2023) and Europe (2022), AARO states that the recordings show apparent thermal signatures with characteristics consistent with physical objects. However, it also concludes that the available information is insufficient to determine whether those signatures represent:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* thermal emission from an actual object;
* thermal reflection;
* environmental heat differences; or
* sensor display error.

</div>

Because the origin of the signature cannot be established, AARO also concludes that the available data cannot reliably evaluate the object's apparent performance characteristics, such as speed or manoeuvring.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/UAP-Cases/Official-UAP-Imagery/quot/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UAP Imagery</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO UAP Imagery...</span></span></span>

This approach reflects a cautious analytical principle. If investigators cannot determine what produced the image, they cannot reliably calculate how a supposed object moved. Apparent acceleration, sudden stops or unusual trajectories may simply arise because the displayed feature was never a discrete object in the first place.

The same reasoning has appeared in AARO's explanations of previously discussed UAP videos. During Senate testimony, AARO Director Jon Kosloski described how one infrared recording initially appeared to show an object entering the water before re-emerging. Further analysis concluded that the apparent "transmedium" behaviour resulted from the infrared camera losing thermal contrast as the object passed over water with a similar temperature, creating an illusion rather than documenting entry into the sea.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.news.com.au/technology/science/space/very-anomalous-objects-pentagon-reveals-bizarre-ufo-sightings-under-investigation/news-story/a41d822643c8faf3a4ebce168b1d1f92" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: news.com.au">[News.com.au]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">news.com.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Black object&#x27;: Pentagon&#x27;s wild UFO revelation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Jon T. ## Why a single video is often insufficient

A single sensor rarely provides enough information to resolve ambiguous imagery.

Important questions often remain unanswered:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* How far away was the apparent target?
* Was it being viewed directly or through atmospheric distortion?
* Was the sensor changing zoom or tracking mode?
* Did other instruments detect the same feature?
* Did radar detect a corresponding object?
* Were weather conditions capable of producing thermal gradients or reflections?

</div>

Without answers to those questions, even experienced analysts may be unable to determine whether a recorded signature corresponds to an aircraft, balloon, bird, atmospheric effect or imaging artefact.

This explains why AARO frequently distinguishes between *unresolved* and *anomalous*. An unresolved case may simply lack sufficient evidence to eliminate competing explanations rather than containing evidence for exotic technology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/UAP-Cases/Official-UAP-Imagery/quot/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UAP Imagery</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO UAP Imagery...</span></span></span>

## What additional evidence can resolve the uncertainty

The uncertainty surrounding these cases is often reduced not by finding a better explanation after the fact, but by obtaining more independent observations of the same event.

The most valuable forms of corroboration include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* simultaneous observations from multiple sensor types, such as infrared together with radar;
* recordings from separate viewing angles that allow triangulation;
* precise telemetry showing camera orientation and aircraft motion;
* environmental measurements including weather, temperature profiles and wind;
* calibrated timing and location metadata;
* longer recordings that capture the object's behaviour before and after the reported event.

</div>

With multiple independent measurements, investigators can determine whether different instruments are tracking the same physical object or whether only one sensor registered an unusual signature. Modern proposals for scientific UAP observation similarly emphasise multimodal sensor systems precisely because independent measurements help distinguish genuine objects from reflections, atmospheric effects and instrument artefacts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18566" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Scientific Investigation of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena (UAP) Using Multimodal Ground-Based ObservatoriesMay 29, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 29, 2023</span></span></span>

## What this means for alleged UFO crashes

Within discussions of alleged UFO crashes, these unresolved sensor cases illustrate an important analytical boundary. If investigators cannot first establish that a recording captured a physical object, the evidence cannot support stronger claims that an object crashed, was recovered or originated from an extraterrestrial source.

An unresolved infrared signature therefore represents an unresolved measurement problem, not evidence of an alien craft. AARO's published assessments consistently separate these possibilities by stating when available data cannot distinguish between a physical object and alternative explanations such as reflections, environmental temperature differences or sensor display errors.
