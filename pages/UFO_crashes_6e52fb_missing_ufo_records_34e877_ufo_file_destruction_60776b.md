## Introduction

[Missing UFO records]({{ 'missing-records/' | relative_url }}) often attract immediate suspicion, especially in discussions of alleged crash retrievals such as [Roswell]({{ 'roswell/' | relative_url }}) or Kecksburg. However, the mere absence of a file is not, by itself, evidence that officials destroyed proof of an extraordinary event. Federal records may disappear for several legitimate reasons, including routine disposal under approved retention schedules, accidental loss, administrative reorganisation or later digitisation. The critical question is not simply whether a file is missing, but **how** it disappeared and whether its destruction complied with legal records-management procedures.

For investigators examining alleged [UFO crashes]({{ 'ufo-crashes/' | relative_url }}), distinguishing routine disposal from suspicious destruction is therefore essential. A documented destruction authorised under federal records schedules generally supports an administrative explanation. By contrast, evidence that records known to exist were removed without authority, selectively destroyed or disappeared during an active investigation raises legitimate questions about governance and accountability. Even then, it demonstrates a records-management problem before it demonstrates a UFO cover-up.

## How federal retention schedules work

Federal agencies in the United States cannot legally destroy records whenever they choose. Under the Federal Records Act, records must be covered by an approved disposition schedule authorised by the National Archives and Records Administration (NARA). Those schedules determine whether records are temporary—eligible for destruction after a specified period—or permanent, requiring preservation and eventual transfer to the National Archives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/records-mgmt/scheduling/sch-records" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Scheduling Records &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesScheduling Records &#124; National ArchivesSeptember 9, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 9, 2025</span></span></span>

## When routine disposal becomes suspicious

A missing file becomes more concerning when the circumstances differ from normal records-management practice rather than merely because the file cannot be found.

### Indicators consistent with routine disposal

Several features generally support an ordinary administrative explanation:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The records fall within an approved temporary records schedule.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.archives.gov/about/records-schedule" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[archives.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NAR A Records Schedule &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NARA Records Schedule &#124; National ArchivesOctober 20, 2021 — NARA Records Schedule Image: National Archives Logo NARA RECORDS SCHEDULE The...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 20, 2021</span></span></span>
* Disposal occurred after the authorised retention period.
* Documentation records who authorised the destruction and when.
* Similar categories of records were destroyed across an entire office or programme rather than only material connected with one incident.

</div>

These characteristics indicate that records disappeared because established policy required it, not because officials were attempting to conceal a particular event.

### Warning signs of possible improper destruction

By contrast, investigators become more concerned when evidence points towards unauthorised disposition rather than scheduled destruction. Potential warning signs include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* Records known to have existed disappear without documented disposal authority.
* Agencies cannot explain what happened to specific files.
* Only records relating to one sensitive incident vanish while comparable files survive.
* Destruction appears to occur after public controversy, litigation or Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) requests begin.
* Internal inventories reference files that later cannot be located.

</div>

None of these factors proves concealment of UFO evidence, but together they strengthen the case for investigating whether records-management rules were violated.

## What counts as unauthorised destruction?

Federal regulations distinguish clearly between authorised destruction and unlawful disposal.

Agencies are required to prevent accidental or unlawful removal or destruction of federal records. Records may only be destroyed under approved NARA schedules, and suspected unauthorised destruction must be reported to the Archivist. NARA maintains formal procedures for investigating allegations of "unauthorised disposition", including situations involving accidental loss, unauthorised deletion or improper destruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/36/1230.10" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.cornell.edu">[cornell.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electronic Code of Federal Regulations (e-CFR) &#124; US Law &#124; LII / Legal Information Institute...</span></span></span>

Importantly, an unauthorised disposal investigation does not automatically imply criminal intent. Cases may involve:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Poor records management.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://foia.state.gov/RecordsManagement/RecordsDisposition2.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: foia.state.gov">[foia.state.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">foia.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Records Disposition2.aspx</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Disposition SchedulesMay 23, 2025 — FOIA Home &gt; Records Management &gt; Records Disposition Schedules RECORDS DISPOSITION SCHEDULES RECORDS...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 23, 2025</span></span></span>
* Office relocations.
* Mislabelled storage.
* Accidental destruction.
* Failure to follow retention procedures.

</div>

NARA's published case files demonstrate that unauthorised disposition investigations occur across many federal agencies—including the U.S. Air Force—for records entirely unrelated to UFOs. Some cases involve missing permanent records, while others concern temporary records destroyed incorrectly. This broader context shows that record losses occur within ordinary government administration and are not unique to controversial subjects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/records-mgmt/resources/unauthorizeddispositionoffederalrecords" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span>

## UFO cases often cited in destruction debates

Discussions surrounding alleged UFO crashes frequently reference missing documentation rather than documented destruction.

For example, researchers examining Roswell have identified incomplete archival collections, inconsistencies between agencies and missing administrative records from the late 1940s. Similar issues arise in discussions of the Kecksburg incident, where investigators have argued that some expected military documentation has not been located.

These examples demonstrate genuine archival gaps. However, in most cases researchers cannot establish one of the key facts necessary to demonstrate suspicious destruction: evidence that a specific file unquestionably existed and was later destroyed outside authorised procedures.

Instead, the available evidence often supports more limited conclusions, such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* records cannot now be located;
* agencies produced inconsistent responses over time;
* documentation may have been lost through ordinary archival processes; or
* retention decisions made decades earlier cannot now be reconstructed.

</div>

That uncertainty limits what historical conclusions can responsibly be drawn.

## The role of FOIA and archival searches

Freedom of Information Act requests often reveal that agencies no longer possess responsive records. This outcome is sometimes interpreted as evidence of concealment, but several different explanations are possible.

An agency may report that:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* records never existed;
* records were transferred elsewhere;
* records were destroyed under an approved schedule;<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.archives.gov/about/records-schedule" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[archives.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NAR A Records Schedule &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NARA Records Schedule &#124; National ArchivesOctober 20, 2021 — NARA Records Schedule Image: National Archives Logo NARA RECORDS SCHEDULE The...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 20, 2021</span></span></span>
* records cannot presently be located; or
* records were improperly lost.

Each response has a different evidential value.

If an agency states that responsive records were destroyed pursuant to an approved records schedule, that generally indicates routine disposal rather than suspicious destruction. If an agency instead reports that records are missing without explanation or that NARA has opened an unauthorised disposition inquiry, the governance issue becomes more significant—but still concerns records management rather than the substantive content of the [missing files]({{ 'missing-files/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/records-mgmt/resources/unauthorizeddispositionoffederalrecords" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span>

## What missing files still cannot prove

Even if investigators established that records relating to an alleged UFO crash were improperly destroyed, several important conclusions would still remain unsupported.

Improper destruction alone cannot demonstrate:

* that the records described an extraterrestrial spacecraft;
* that officials recovered non-human technology;
* that [witnesses]({{ 'witnesses/' | relative_url }})' recollections were accurate;
* that later government statements were knowingly false; or
* that a coordinated decades-long cover-up existed.

Those claims require independent corroborating evidence, such as surviving documents, physical material, authenticated photographs, contemporaneous witness testimony or multiple independent archival sources.

In other words, suspicious destruction may strengthen the case for questioning official record-keeping, but it does not establish what the missing records contained.

## A balanced standard for judging missing UFO files

The strongest historical approach is to separate three increasingly serious questions:

1. **Did records once exist?** Independent evidence such as inventories, file indexes or correspondence can sometimes establish this.
2. **Were they destroyed properly?** Records schedules, disposal documentation and NARA guidance help answer this question.
3. **What did they contain?** This requires surviving evidence beyond the fact that the records disappeared.

Only the first two questions concern records management. The third concerns historical proof.

For debates over alleged UFO crashes, this distinction is crucial. Routine disposal generally reflects ordinary federal administration. Unauthorised destruction may indicate failures in governance or accountability. Neither outcome, standing alone, demonstrates that missing files concealed evidence of an extraterrestrial crash.
