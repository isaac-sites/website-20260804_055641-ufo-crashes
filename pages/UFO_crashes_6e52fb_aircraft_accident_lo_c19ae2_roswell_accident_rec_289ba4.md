## Introduction

Claims that a [secret aircraft]({{ 'secret-aircraft/' | relative_url }}) crash near [Roswell]({{ 'roswell/' | relative_url }}) was erased from the historical record often rest on one central argument: if the paperwork is missing, the government could have hidden the event. Federal investigations into the surviving records reached a more limited conclusion. They found that some Roswell Army Air Field administrative files were indeed destroyed under unclear circumstances, but they also found no contemporary accident report, operational record or inter-agency documentation matching the alleged crash of a crewed aircraft or an unknown vehicle. Instead, the surviving records consistently point towards the recovery of balloon-related debris rather than an aircraft with occupants.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-95-187.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeNSIAD-95-187 Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell...</span></span></span>

The distinction matters because missing records and evidence of a concealed crash are not the same thing. The surviving documentary record is incomplete, yet the available accident files, military reporting requirements and contemporaneous documents provide a basis for assessing what would ordinarily have existed had a military aircraft crashed near Roswell in July 1947.

## What the federal records search examined

In 1994, at the request of Congressman Steven Schiff, the U.S. General Accounting Office (now the Government Accountability Office, or GAO) undertook an unusually broad search for records relating to the Roswell incident. The investigation extended beyond Air Force files to include material from the Department of Defense, FBI, CIA, National Security Council and other agencies. It also examined records-management practices to determine whether relevant documents should have survived.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeGovernment Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell, New Mexico &#124;...</span></span></span>

The GAO focused on two practical questions:

* whether Army regulations in 1947 required reports for [aircraft accidents]({{ 'aircraft-accidents/' | relative_url }});
* whether any government records described an aircraft crash near Roswell that matched later claims.

The investigation concluded that Army regulations required reports on military aircraft accidents and that such reports were intended to be retained permanently. By contrast, officials stated there was no comparable reporting requirement for the recovery of a weather balloon or similar balloon equipment. That distinction became important because it meant that the absence of an aircraft accident report was significant only if an aircraft had actually crashed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-95-187.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeNSIAD-95-187 Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell...</span></span></span>

The search recovered only two contemporaneous [1947 records]({{ '1947-records/' | relative_url }}) directly referring to the Roswell event:

* a July 1947 history compiled by the 509th Bomb Group and Roswell Army Air Field stating that the recovered "flying disc" was determined to be a radar-tracking balloon; and
* an FBI teletype dated 8 July 1947 reporting that military personnel had recovered an object resembling a high-altitude weather balloon with a radar reflector.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-95-187.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeNSIAD-95-187 Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell...</span></span></span>

No additional classified or previously withheld records examined by the GAO described a hidden aircraft crash or the recovery of an unknown craft. Project [Blue Book]({{ 'blue-book/' | relative_url }})'s historical files likewise contain no documentation of the 1947 Roswell incident because the project itself began years later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-95-187.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeNSIAD-95-187 Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell...</span></span></span>

## The documented New Mexico crashes of July 1947

One way to test the hidden-crash hypothesis is to compare it with documented aviation accidents from the same period.

The GAO reviewed the Air Force Safety Agency's accident records and identified four military aircraft accidents in New Mexico during July 1947. All occurred after 8 July, the date on which Roswell Army Air Field publicly announced the recovery of a "flying disc." These accidents involved conventional military aircraft near Hobbs, Albuquerque, Carrizozo and Alamogordo. Only one was fatal. None matched later accounts of a mysterious crash outside Roswell, and none involved an [unidentified]({{ 'unidentified/' | relative_url }}) vehicle. The Navy reported no aircraft accidents in New Mexico during that month.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-95-187.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeNSIAD-95-187 Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell...</span></span></span>

This finding does not prove that every accident would necessarily have been recorded perfectly. It does show, however, that the military accident-reporting system was functioning and that accidents elsewhere in New Mexico generated the expected documentation. The absence of a comparable Roswell accident report therefore requires an explanation.

The official explanation advanced by the Air Force was that no aircraft accident occurred because the recovered material came from a balloon system rather than a military aeroplane. Whether one accepts the specific Project MOGUL identification or not, the reporting distinction remains central: balloon recoveries were not processed under the same accident-reporting rules as crashed aircraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-95-187.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeNSIAD-95-187 Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell...</span></span></span>

## Why missing files do not establish a concealed crash

The strongest argument advanced by proponents of a concealed crash is that important Roswell records no longer exist.

The GAO confirmed that some records had indeed been destroyed. Among the missing material were Roswell Army Air Field administrative records covering 1945–1949 and outgoing message files from 1946–1949. The disposition forms available to investigators did not identify precisely who authorised their destruction or under what authority it occurred.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-95-187.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeNSIAD-95-187 Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell...</span></span></span>

Those losses are genuine historical gaps, but several factors limit what they demonstrate.

First, the destroyed files were broad categories of administrative records rather than a known aircraft accident file. There is no evidence that a specific crash investigation once existed and was selectively removed.

Second, investigators searched many independent record systems outside Roswell itself. If a major military aircraft crash involving recovery operations had occurred, it would ordinarily have generated related records elsewhere, including accident investigations, headquarters correspondence, logistics, intelligence reporting or command communications. The GAO found no such documentary trail.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-95-187.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeNSIAD-95-187 Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell...</span></span></span>

Third, surviving contemporaneous documents consistently describe balloon-related debris rather than an aircraft. While those documents do not answer every question raised over subsequent decades, they do provide positive evidence that contrasts with the argument based solely on absent files.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-95-187.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeNSIAD-95-187 Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell...</span></span></span>

This illustrates an important historical principle: an incomplete archive creates uncertainty, but uncertainty alone does not establish a particular alternative explanation. Missing paperwork may prevent historians from reconstructing every administrative detail, yet it cannot by itself demonstrate that an undocumented aircraft carrying a crew crashed near Roswell.

## Assessing the missing-crash claim

Within the broader history of UFO [crash narratives]({{ 'crash-narrative/' | relative_url }}), Roswell occupies an unusual position because genuine archival losses coexist with surviving records that point in a different direction.

The documentary evidence currently available supports three cautious conclusions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* some Roswell administrative records from the late 1940s no longer survive;
* federal investigators found no contemporary aircraft accident report corresponding to a hidden crash near Roswell;
* the surviving 1947 records consistently describe recovery of balloon-related material rather than an aircraft or recovered bodies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-95-187.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeNSIAD-95-187 Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell...</span></span></span>

</div>

For that reason, the argument that a secret Roswell aircraft crash was "erased from the records" goes beyond what the documentary evidence presently supports. The historical record contains genuine gaps, but the available accident files and federal records searches have not uncovered evidence of the missing aircraft investigation that the concealment theory would predict.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-95-187.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeNSIAD-95-187 Government Records: Results of a Search for Records Concerning the 1947 Crash Near Roswell...</span></span></span>
